# Shark Tank India — Season 5, Episode 24
## *Brewing Deals in the Tank*

**Sharks Present:** Aman Gupta · Namita Thapar · Kanika Tekriwal · Anupam Mittal · Vineeta Singh

**Deals Closed:** 1 of 3 (Wholeleaf ✅ · Chewie/Mankomb ❌ · Madrasi Kaapi House ❌)

> **What this episode really teaches:** A grieving nephew who turned a family medical crisis into a government-licensed cannabis wellness company — backed by 13 clinical trials. Three IIT/IIM alumni who built a deeptech kitchen appliance in 11 months and walked in pre-revenue with a ₹133 crore valuation ask. And two neighbours who spent five years perfecting South Indian filter coffee only to face the oldest lesson in food & beverage: great coffee isn't the same as a great business. Episode 24 is about the gap between personal conviction and investor conviction — and what it takes to close it.

## FOUNDER PROFILES — WHO BUILT THESE BUSINESSES AND WHAT IT TOOK

---

### WHOLELEAF — Shivraj Sharma
**Age at founding:** ~30 · **Education:** Purdue University (USA) · **Based in:** Delhi / Gurugram

Shivraj Sharma is a Purdue-educated entrepreneur — one of India's premier engineering universities' American equivalent — who returned to India not with a startup idea but with a problem he couldn't stop thinking about. His founding story is entirely personal and unusually honest: in 2009, his aunt was diagnosed with breast cancer. During her chemotherapy, Shivraj watched her lose her appetite, manage unbearable pain, and struggle with the side effects that often make treatment feel worse than the disease itself. Within 10 days of trying CBD oil derived from the Vijaya (cannabis) plant, her appetite improved and pain levels dropped meaningfully. That observation became a decade-long obsession.

Shivraj didn't act immediately. He spent years learning — reading global research on cannabinoids, travelling overseas to meet manufacturers and scientists working with cannabis formulations in countries where it was already accepted medically, and understanding India's legal landscape (specifically the NDPS Act 1985 which permits cannabis *leaf and seed* extracts while prohibiting the flower and resin — the distinction that makes Wholeleaf's product legal). He then watched other family members with chronic pain, sleep disorders, and anxiety respond to Vijaya-based therapies and become functional again.

The business was born in 2020 — a decade after the aunt's cancer battle — because Shivraj was ready: he had the scientific literacy, the regulatory understanding, the supplier relationships, and crucially, the personal conviction that no amount of market research could replicate. He was not founding a company in a category he had identified through data. He was solving a problem he had lived inside for ten years.

**What his background explains about the business:** Purdue's engineering rigour is visible in how Wholeleaf was built — clinical trials (13 of them, each with 50–75 participants) before commercial scale, GMP-certified manufacturing before aggressive marketing, Apollo Pharmacy partnerships before premium D2C ambitions. This is a scientist-entrepreneur's approach: validate the product before selling the story. Most consumer wellness founders do the opposite. Shivraj's willingness to invest in clinical evidence at early stage is both his credibility engine and the reason 76% of trial participants reported significant relief — a statistic that is genuinely difficult to manufacture.

**The personal dimension that shapes the pitch:** When Shivraj walks into the Tank and says cannabis helped his aunt through chemotherapy, it isn't a marketing narrative. It's a founding fact that every investor in the room can feel is true. Authentic origin stories are investment assets precisely because they explain founder persistence — why this person will still be running this company at 3AM when the regulatory environment shifts again, or when a new batch of clinical trials is needed. Shivraj's decade of commitment before founding is the answer.

**Founded:** July 2024 · **Based in:** Bengaluru (HSR Layout)

**Mrudul Mudotholy (Co-Founder & CEO)** is an engineer by training (BE from University Visvesvaraya College of Engineering, Bengaluru — one of Karnataka's oldest and most respected engineering institutions) with a postgraduate degree from IIM Kozhikode, one of India's premier management schools. His career spans 15+ years in deep-tech, mobility, and industrial AI — specifically at Bosch Group (Robert Bosch India, Bosch Global Software Technologies, and Robert Bosch Wuxi in China) and Myelin Foundry, an AI startup focused on edge computing. He holds multiple patents and won the Sir M. Visvesvaraya Centenary Award — a prestigious recognition in Karnataka's engineering community named after the legendary engineer and statesman. Before Mankomb, he was quietly building what he describes as a "stealth startup" — suggesting Chewie was conceived in private before its public launch.

**Dinesh Babu Sukumar (Co-Founder & CMO)** studied at Vellore Institute of Technology (VIT) for his engineering undergraduate and the University of East London for his postgraduate degree — giving him both an Indian engineering foundation and international market exposure. He has 14+ years of experience in marketing for deep-tech and biotech companies, most recently as Senior Product Marketing Manager at Myelin Foundry (where he worked alongside Mrudul). He previously founded Aidplus Medical — meaning Dinesh is a second-time founder, not a first-timer. His go-to-market experience spans India, the GCC (Gulf Cooperation Council countries), and the UK — geographic knowledge that directly informs Chewie's international expansion plans (FY 28–29 UK market entry).

**Jawahar Arumugam (Co-Founder & CTO)** brings 20+ years of R&D and solution architecture leadership — the most technically experienced of the three and the person most responsible for Chewie's AI vision system, composting chamber engineering, and the real-time sensor integration that makes the product function. His background in solution architecture means he thinks in systems — how does every component talk to every other component, where does the pipeline break, how do you build robustness into hardware that has to work reliably in Indian kitchen conditions (variable temperature, humidity, power fluctuations).

**What their collective background explains:** Mrudul (IIM Kozhikode + Bosch + AI patents), Dinesh (VIT + UEL + Myelin Foundry + prior startup), and Jawahar (20+ years R&D) constitute a team of rare combined experience. The product reflects this: Chewie is not a simple composter — it is an AI-native device with on-device computer vision, real-time sensor control, and proprietary composting algorithms. Building this in 11 months (July 2024 to the Shark Tank pitch) with 9 people and ₹40 lakh in development spend is genuinely impressive. The team's corporate AI experience (Bosch's industrial automation, Myelin Foundry's edge AI) gave them a toolkit that most hardware startups spend years acquiring.

**The gap their background creates:** Deep-tech pedigree is also the source of Mankomb's pitch problem. Engineers and AI researchers are trained to solve problems completely before presenting them — but investors fund momentum, not perfection. A team this credentialled could have entered the Tank with 100 pre-orders, concrete pilot data, and narrowed use-case positioning. Instead, they came pre-revenue with a ₹133 crore valuation and global ambitions across consumer homes, corporate offices, and international markets. The very expertise that built a great product created the overconfidence that priced it out of the room.

### MADRASI KAAPI HOUSE — Venkata Shiva, Simran Venkat & Smita Rohan Alke
**Founded:** 2019 · **Based in:** Mumbai

The founding of Madrasi Kaapi House is one of the simplest origin stories in Episode 24 — and sometimes the simplest stories are the most honest.

**Venkata Shiva** and **Simran Venkat** are neighbours who became business partners — a friendship that turned into a business, which is both its strength (deep trust, shared daily context) and a structural consideration (friends who become co-founders don't always separate emotional decisions from operational ones cleanly). Venkata Shiva brings the cultural rootedness: as a South Indian, filter coffee is not an acquired taste or a market opportunity — it is morning, it is home, it is a ritual performed daily in his family for generations. His understanding of what authentic filter coffee tastes like, what decoction strength it requires, and what the experience should feel like is not learnable from a menu research exercise.

**Smita Rohan Alke** joined as the third co-founder, rounding out a team that combines cultural product knowledge (Venkata), business partnership roots (Simran), and operational depth (Smita). The three of them identified the same problem independently: South Indian filter coffee — arguably India's most sophisticated and regionally beloved coffee culture — had almost no credible, scalable QSR representation in Mumbai. The city runs on cutting chai and third-wave espresso; between those two poles, a market for authentic filter coffee in a quick-service format sat entirely unserved.

Their model is chef-less and process-driven — the standardisation that most QSR brands take years to develop was baked in from the beginning. A single brew produces 30+ beverage varieties, the outlet doesn't require a trained barista, and the process is documented tightly enough to franchise. This thinking — standardise before scaling — reflects operational maturity uncommon in three-person food startup teams.

**What their background explains about both the success and the failure:** The cultural rootedness produced an authentic, high-quality product. The neighbour-partnership foundation produced operational trust and cost discipline (revenue grew from ₹8 lakh in FY20 to ₹2.5 crore in FY25 with virtually no wasted capital). But what the founding team lacked — and what became the fatal pitch gap — was the strategic map for national scaling. Knowing how to make the best South Indian filter coffee in Mumbai is not the same as knowing how to systematically penetrate a market dominated by Café Coffee Day's historical presence and Third Wave Coffee's premium positioning. The Sharks wanted to see that map. The team had a product, not yet a plan.

### WHAT ALL THREE FOUNDING STORIES SHARE

Episode 24's founders come from dramatically different contexts: a Purdue-educated son responding to a family cancer battle, a trio of IIM/IIT-adjacent AI engineers building deep sustainability tech, and two Mumbai neighbours trying to bring their coffee culture to a city that didn't yet know it wanted it. But all three share the same founding DNA: **personal immersion preceded commercial intention.**

Shivraj didn't study the CBD market and decide to enter it. He watched his aunt heal and couldn't build anything else. Mrudul didn't research the home appliance sector and find a gap. He spent 15 years building AI systems and believed they could solve everyday sustainability problems. Venkata didn't analyse the Mumbai café market and identify filter coffee as underserved. He missed it, made it at home, and decided others should have it too.

This pattern — living the problem before building the solution — is the most reliable predictor of founder authenticity that investors (and audiences) can assess. The Sharks didn't question whether any of these founders believed in what they were building. What they questioned was whether the founders had translated their personal conviction into a business architecture that could produce investor returns. That gap — between personal conviction and commercial architecture — is the defining lesson of Episode 24.

## PITCH 1 — WHOLELEAF
### *From a Family Cancer Battle to India's First Clinically Validated Cannabis Wellness Brand*

---

### 1. Business Snapshot

Wholeleaf is a Delhi-based, government-licensed cannabis wellness company founded in 2020 by Shivraj Sharma. It extracts CBD (Cannabidiol) and full-spectrum cannabinoids from the **Vijaya plant leaf** — not the flower or bud — which places it squarely within the legal framework of India's NDPS Act 1985. The brand harmonises traditional Ayurveda with modern clinical science, targeting chronic pain, anxiety, and sleep disorders through formulations that have been through 13 independent clinical trials.

**The legal distinction that makes Wholeleaf possible:** India's NDPS Act prohibits cannabis resin and flowers (high THC concentration, psychoactive) but explicitly permits cannabis *leaf and seed* extracts for medical and Ayurvedic purposes. Vijaya has been an ingredient in Ayurvedic medicine for centuries — referenced in the Atharvaveda and Ayurvedic pharmacopoeias. Wholeleaf's legal foundation rests on this ancient-modern continuity: Vijaya leaf extract is simultaneously an Ayurvedic ingredient and a source of CBD, making it lawful under both the NDPS Act and the Drugs and Cosmetics Act with the appropriate AYUSH licensing. This is not a grey area — Wholeleaf holds the requisite government licences, which is their moat against competitors who don't want to navigate the same regulatory complexity.

---

### 2. Products & What They Solve

Wholeleaf addresses the **chronic pain crisis** that India's conventional healthcare system treats with opioids, NSAIDs, and long-term pharmaceuticals — each carrying significant side effects and dependency risks. Its products offer a plant-based alternative with clinical evidence, not just wellness claims.

**Hero Products:**

- **Orthodexil:** Formulated specifically for arthritis and inflammation. Targets the 18 crore+ Indians living with joint pain (arthritis is India's most common chronic condition). Combines CBD with Ayurvedic anti-inflammatory compounds. Offered as an oral tincture for systemic absorption.

- **Migroheal:** Targets migraines and recurring headaches. Migraine affects approximately 15 crore Indians and is among the most undertreated pain conditions — neurologists are scarce, pharmaceutical options are expensive or carry dependency risks, and most sufferers manage with over-the-counter painkillers that lose efficacy over repeated use.

- **Anxiety and Sleep formulations:** Oral tinctures for stress-related insomnia and anxiety disorders. Addresses the urban professional's most common, least-diagnosed health burden — the anxiety-insomnia loop that affects an estimated 5 crore urban Indians.

**Full-Spectrum Vijaya extracts:** Wholeleaf uses full-spectrum rather than CBD isolate. This matters because full-spectrum formulations preserve the "entourage effect" — the synergistic interaction of multiple cannabinoids, terpenes, and flavonoids that produces better outcomes than CBD alone. This formulation choice reflects genuine scientific literacy, not just regulatory compliance.

**The clinical trial asset:** Wholeleaf is the first Indian company in this space to have conducted 13 clinical trials, each involving 50–75 participants, showing 76% of participants experienced significant pain or symptom relief. This evidence base is not window dressing — it is the brand's core differentiator in a market saturated with wellness claims backed by nothing but testimonials. Clinical evidence enables Apollo Pharmacy stocking, enables physician recommendations, and enables a conversation with regulators that no amount of social media marketing can replace.

---

### 3. Numbers That Matter

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Founded | 2020 |
| April 2024 Revenue | Near zero |
| ARR (October 2025) | ₹9 crore |
| Growth in 18 months | 16.5x |
| Gross Margin | 75% |
| EBITDA Margin | -17% |
| COGS | 25% of revenue |
| Marketing Spend | 49% of expenditure |
| Logistics + Commissions | 13% |
| Overhead | 30% |
| Online Revenue Share | 60% (85% own website, 15% Amazon) |
| Offline Revenue Share | 40% (90% via Apollo Pharmacy) |
| Apollo Pharmacy presence | 600 retail stores nationwide |

**Reading the -17% EBITDA in context:** A 75% gross margin is exceptional — it means the product is highly profitable before operating expenses. The -17% EBITDA is entirely driven by a 49% marketing spend — a deliberate, time-limited investment in category creation and consumer education. This is not a cost control failure; it is a growth strategy. Building consumer awareness and physician trust in a regulated, stigmatised category (cannabis) requires sustained, intensive marketing spend. Brands that exit this phase with loyal customers and clinical credibility — Wholeleaf's trajectory suggests this is where they're heading — can then compress marketing spend and let the 75% gross margin flow to profit rapidly.

**The 16.5x growth in 18 months** is the pitch's most compelling number. Near-zero to ₹9 crore ARR in 18 months, in a regulated industry with no mass-market advertising (cannabis products cannot be advertised on mainstream platforms), through a D2C-first and Apollo Pharmacy model, is extraordinary. It means the product works, the distribution is functioning, and repeat customers are coming back — all without the advertising tailwinds that conventional wellness brands rely on.

---

### 4. Original Ask vs. Final Deal

| | Ask | Received |
|---|---|---|
| Amount | ₹50 lakh | ₹1.5 crore |
| Equity | 2.1% | 7.5% |
| Valuation | ₹23.8 crore | ₹20 crore |
| Investors | — | **Aman Gupta + Namita Thapar + Kanika Tekriwal** |

**The deal structure is striking:** Shivraj asked for ₹50 lakh and walked out with ₹1.5 crore — 3x the capital he sought, at a modest valuation compression (₹23.8 crore to ₹20 crore, just 16% haircut). Three Sharks co-invested. The equity given (7.5%) is proportionate to the capital received — a fair exchange.

**Why three Sharks co-invested:** This is unusual and signals high conviction from multiple directions. Aman's consumer brand scaling expertise, Namita's pharma distribution and healthcare credibility (crucial for a medically-adjacent product), and Kanika Tekriwal's personal resonance with the brand (JetSetGo's founder background in a regulated, stigmatised industry gives her a particular appreciation for what Shivraj has navigated). Together they bring: distribution access, medical credibility, and regulatory navigation experience.

**Why competitive offers led to this outcome:** Multiple Sharks made offers, creating the negotiating dynamic that shifted from ₹50 lakh to ₹1.5 crore. Shivraj's willingness to accept a syndicate deal at 3x capital reflects sound thinking: in a capital-intensive category requiring clinical trials, regulatory compliance, and physician relationship-building, more capital at a slightly lower valuation is almost always better than less capital at a higher valuation.

---

### 5. Sharks' Logic

**Namita's investment thesis:** As the former ED of Emcure Pharmaceuticals (a ₹7,000+ crore pharma company), Namita understands that the path from Ayurvedic wellness claim to physician-recommended product requires clinical evidence, regulatory navigation, and pharmacy distribution relationships. Wholeleaf has all three in early form — 13 trials, government licensing, and Apollo (600 stores). She is investing in a business that speaks her language.

**Aman's thesis:** Consumer brand + Apollo Pharmacy distribution + clinical proof = a brand that can be built through digital channels while being validated through medical ones. This is the dual-channel consumer health playbook he understands from D2C scaling experience.

**Kanika's thesis:** She has built JetSetGo in a regulated, non-obvious category (private aviation in India) that most investors dismissed early. She recognises in Wholeleaf the same pattern: legitimate, documented benefit in a category carrying unfair stigma, with a founder committed to regulatory correctness rather than regulatory circumvention.

---

### 6. Key Business Terms in Action

**Category Creation in Regulated Markets:** Building a product category where consumer awareness is low and regulatory environment is complex requires a different playbook than entering an established market. You cannot advertise on Google or Meta (cannabis-adjacent keywords are restricted). You cannot use celebrity endorsements freely. You cannot rely on impulse purchase behaviour. Instead, category creation happens through: physician education (doctors who understand the science recommend the product), clinical evidence (trials that produce publishable data), and institutional distribution (Apollo Pharmacy stocking legitimises the product for every skeptical consumer). Wholeleaf has invested in all three. The 49% marketing spend that appears high is partly the cost of this category-creation investment.

**The 75% Gross Margin — What It Means for Scaling:** Every rupee of revenue Wholeleaf generates leaves 75 paise after manufacturing costs. This is exceptional for a physical product (consumer goods typically run 40–60% gross margins). It means that as marketing spend eventually normalises (from 49% to perhaps 20–25% as brand recognition grows), EBITDA can swing from -17% to +30–40% with the same revenue base. Investors who understand this dynamic back high-gross-margin businesses even at negative EBITDA, because the path to profitability is visible and arithmetic rather than speculative.

**B2B2C through Apollo Pharmacy:** The Apollo Group partnership is not just a distribution channel — it's a credibility certification. Apollo's pharmacy buyers evaluate every product they stock for safety, regulatory compliance, and demonstrated demand. The fact that Wholeleaf is on Apollo's shelves tells every skeptical consumer: this product has been vetted by one of India's most trusted healthcare institutions. The 30-day credit cycle that Apollo operates on (Wholeleaf ships, gets paid 30 days later) creates working capital requirements — part of why the ₹1.5 crore investment is necessary. More capital = more inventory = more Apollo shelf presence = more revenue.

---

### 7. Market Opportunity & Strategic Context

**India's Pain Management Market:** $10–12 billion (₹83,000–1,00,000 crore), making it one of the largest single-category healthcare markets in the country. 19% of Indian adults — approximately 27 crore people — suffer from chronic pain. Current treatment options are dominated by NSAIDs (which cause GI damage with prolonged use), opioids (addiction risk), and physiotherapy (expensive, time-intensive). A safe, effective, plant-based alternative with clinical evidence addresses an enormous, underserved market.

**The Adjacent Markets:**
- **Ayurvedic market:** ₹1,20,000 crore growing at 15–20% CAGR — Wholeleaf fits squarely within this growth trajectory
- **Global medical cannabis:** Projected $50 billion by 2030 — India as early adopter (with existing Vijaya legal framework) has significant first-mover advantage
- **Premium natural/herbal pain segment:** $1.5 billion, growing faster than conventional pharma pain management

**TAM → SAM → SOM:**
- **TAM:** India's pain management market — ₹83,000+ crore
- **SAM:** Chronic pain patients, stressed professionals, and sleep-disorder sufferers open to Ayurvedic/natural alternatives — ₹5,000–8,000 crore
- **SOM (5-year):** ₹100–300 crore — achievable with Apollo Pharmacy expansion (600 → 6,000 stores), physician education programs, and sustained D2C growth

**Target Audience (Four Segments):**
1. **Chronic pain patients (45–70):** Arthritis, neuropathy, cancer pain — the primary clinical cohort where evidence-based alternatives to pharmaceuticals are most urgently needed
2. **Stressed urban professionals (25–45):** Insomnia, anxiety, burnout — the largest volume segment, addressable through D2C digital channels
3. **Fitness enthusiasts:** Natural recovery without NSAIDs — a growing segment that already understands CBD from international exposure
4. **Eco-conscious consumers:** Plant-based medicine as philosophy — smaller segment but highest advocacy behaviour

**Distribution Strategy:**
- **Online (60%):** Own D2C website (85% of online) + Amazon (15%). Physician-recommendation-driven traffic is highest converting
- **Offline (40%):** Apollo Pharmacy (90% of offline). Target: expand from 600 to 6,000+ Apollo stores + add independent medical stores and hospital pharmacies
- **B2B Healthcare:** Hospitals, oncology clinics, pain management specialists — the highest-value channel for chronic pain formulations

**Advantages:**
- Only Indian company with 13 clinical trials in this space — regulatory and scientific moat
- Government-licensed — significant barrier to entry
- Apollo Pharmacy partnership — institutional distribution credibility
- 75% gross margin — exceptional unit economics
- 16.5x growth in 18 months — market demand validation

**Challenges:**
- -17% EBITDA driven by 49% marketing spend — burning cash aggressively
- Consumer stigma around cannabis in India — education burden is expensive
- Advertising restrictions — cannot use mainstream digital ad channels freely
- Regulatory uncertainty — policy changes could affect the NDPS interpretation
- Single founder structure — no disclosed co-founder; execution concentration risk

**Long-term Vision:** Become India's leading evidence-based cannabis wellness company — the brand that physicians recommend, pharmacies stock, and patients trust. At ₹100+ crore revenue with normalised marketing spend (~20%), EBITDA margins of 30%+ are achievable. Strategic exit options include acquisition by a major Ayurvedic conglomerate (Dabur, Himalaya), a pharmaceutical company seeking wellness diversification, or global medical cannabis players entering India.

---

### 8. Takeaway for Learners

**Clinical evidence is the most expensive moat to build and the hardest to copy.** Thirteen clinical trials with 50–75 participants each represent years of investment and regulatory navigation. The payoff: Wholeleaf can stand in front of Apollo Pharmacy buyers, physicians, and Sharks and say "76% of participants reported significant relief" — a claim that cannot be manufactured. In regulated health categories, evidence is the product's most important feature.

**Personal mission sustains founders through regulatory complexity.** Building a cannabis wellness company in India requires navigating the NDPS Act, AYUSH licensing, state excise regulations, digital advertising restrictions, and constant stakeholder skepticism. Shivraj's decade-long personal commitment — born from watching his aunt heal — is the only credible answer to "why would you do this?" when the regulatory environment is this challenging. Investors back founders who cannot do anything else.

**High gross margin + temporary high burn = a specific kind of investor opportunity.** Wholeleaf's -17% EBITDA at 75% gross margin is not a broken business — it's a category-creation business in investment mode. Sophisticated investors read these numbers differently from naive ones. The naive reading: "losing money, avoid." The sophisticated reading: "exceptional unit economics masked by deliberate category-building spend; the moment marketing normalises, this becomes highly profitable." Learn to present and defend this distinction.

**Three Sharks co-investing is a signal, not just a result.** When three experienced investors independently reach the same conviction, it validates the investment thesis more than any single investor's opinion. For Wholeleaf, the Aman + Namita + Kanika syndicate covers consumer scaling, medical credibility, and regulated-industry navigation — precisely the three capabilities this specific business needs most.

## PITCH 2 — CHEWIE (MANKOMB TECHNOLOGIES)
### *Brilliant Deep-Tech, Wrong Valuation, Missing Traction — A Pre-Revenue Pitch Decoded*

---

### 1. Business Snapshot

Chewie is an **AI-powered home wet waste management appliance** developed by Mankomb Technologies Private Limited, a Bengaluru-based deep-tech startup founded in July 2024. The product converts household organic waste (vegetable peels, fruit waste, food scraps — the "wet waste" that Indian households generate daily and which constitutes 50–60% of total municipal solid waste) into nutrient-rich compost soil in 8–40 hours using on-device computer vision, automated composting chamber control, and real-time sensor management.

The brand name "Mankomb" is derived from "Manga Kombu" — the Dravidian term for mango stem, which naturally preserves the fruit's freshness. The name signals: we preserve what matters, we're rooted in our culture, and sustainability is not a buzzword for us.

---

### 2. Product & What It Solves

**India's wet waste problem:** India generates approximately 62 million tonnes of solid waste annually. Wet waste (organic kitchen waste) comprises 50–60% of this — roughly 30–37 million tonnes per year. Municipal systems cannot process this volume. Wet waste in landfills generates methane (a greenhouse gas 25x more potent than CO2) and leachate that contaminates groundwater. At the household level, wet waste is the most consistently unpleasant daily chore — sticky, smelly, and often generating fruit flies before the weekly municipal pickup.

**How Chewie works:**
- Household organic waste is loaded into the composting chamber
- On-device AI vision identifies waste types and optimises the processing parameters
- Real-time sensors monitor temperature, airflow, and moisture levels
- The system automatically adjusts to maximise decomposition speed and quality
- Output: nutrient-rich compost soil in 8–40 hours (depending on waste type and volume)
- The resulting soil can be used for home plants, terrace gardens, or donated to community green spaces

**The AI differentiation:** What separates Chewie from passive composters (which are simply enclosed bins where decomposition happens slowly and inconsistently) is the active AI management. A passive composter in Indian conditions (variable temperature, high humidity, irregular waste input) produces inconsistent output — sometimes good compost, often a smelly, unprocessed mess. Chewie's AI-controlled chamber produces consistent, usable compost reliably. This reliability is the product's core commercial promise: sustainability that actually works in the real world, not just in ideal conditions.

**Pricing:** ₹34,999 per unit — positioning Chewie as a premium kitchen appliance, comparable to a high-end air purifier or water purifier. This price point targets the SEC-A urban household (₹20+ lakh annual income) for whom the product is both affordable and aspirational.

---

### 3. Numbers That Matter

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Founded | July 2024 |
| Ask | ₹2 crore for 1.5% equity |
| Implied Valuation | ₹133.3 crore |
| Pre-bookings | 1,000 units |
| Revenue from pre-bookings | ~₹10 lakh (advance booking fees) |
| Gross Margin (on pre-booked units) | ~40% |
| Net Margin | -27% (after SG&A and direct costs) |
| Total development spend | ₹40 lakh |
| Team size | 9 members |
| Unit MRP | ₹34,999 |
| FY 25-26 target | 1,000 units |
| FY 26-27 target | 10,000 units |
| International expansion | UK (FY 28-29) |

**The valuation problem stated plainly:** ₹133.3 crore valuation on ₹10 lakh in advance booking fees. That's a 1,333x revenue multiple. Even accounting for the pre-revenue stage, the credentialled team, and the defensible technology, this multiple requires extraordinary justification — specifically, evidence that the 1,000 pre-bookings will convert to paid sales, that the 40% gross margin is achievable at scale, and that the path to 10,000 units in FY27 is operationally credible. None of these could be proven in the pitch room.

**The -27% net margin on pre-booked units:** Even at early small-batch manufacturing, net margins are negative. This is normal for hardware startups — manufacturing at 1,000 units has none of the cost advantages of manufacturing at 10,000 units. But it makes the ₹133 crore valuation even harder to defend: negative margins at small scale, ambitious volume targets, and pre-revenue status is a combination that requires extraordinary investor faith.

---

### 4. Final Deal

**❌ No Deal — All Sharks passed**

**Why the valuation was the central problem:** A ₹133 crore valuation requires a credible path to ₹600–1,300 crore in future enterprise value (assuming Sharks target 5–10x returns). For Chewie to reach ₹600 crore enterprise value, it would need to become a dominant player in the Indian home appliance market — which requires competing against established players (Prestige, Butterfly, Bajaj Electricals) with mature distribution and brand trust, while also achieving the manufacturing scale that makes ₹34,999 a sustainable price point rather than a small-batch premium. That is not impossible — but at pre-revenue stage, it requires the founders to argue entirely from potential, with no present-day evidence. Sharks don't invest in potential without proof.

**The category scaling problem:** Chewie faces a version of the Indian sustainability consumer paradox: the people who care most about composting are also the people who are most likely to do it manually (in a balcony composter bin for ₹500). Converting the mass-market SEC-A consumer who doesn't currently compost into a Chewie buyer at ₹34,999 requires a behaviour change investment that is expensive and slow. The Sharks' unspoken concern: is the target customer the sustainability enthusiast who will buy regardless, or the mainstream household who doesn't yet know they need this? The former is a small market; the latter requires category creation capital Mankomb doesn't have.

---

### 5. Sharks' Logic (Why Each Passed)

**Anupam's likely position:** Pre-revenue hardware at ₹133 crore is a bet on a team, not a business. The team is excellent — but team bets at this valuation require either exceptional early traction or proprietary technology with proven commercial demand. 1,000 pre-bookings (advance fees, not full payments) are encouraging but insufficient evidence at ₹133 crore.

**Vineeta's likely position:** Consumer appliances require retail distribution at scale. Chewie at ₹34,999 is a premium product that needs to sit in physical stores where customers can see, touch, and understand it. Building that distribution for a single-SKU product from a brand-new company is capital-intensive and slow. The ₹2 crore ask doesn't fund the distribution buildout that success requires.

**Aman's likely position:** He has seen the hardware startup graveyard — companies that had excellent products but couldn't navigate the inventory, logistics, and after-sales service complexity of physical appliances at scale. Without evidence that Chewie has solved the manufacturing-at-scale challenge and the distribution puzzle, the investment risk is too high relative to the ask valuation.

---

### 6. Key Business Terms in Action

**Hardware Startup Valuation at Pre-Revenue Stage:** Hardware valuations are structurally harder to defend than software valuations because hardware has manufacturing costs, inventory risk, quality control complexity, after-sales service obligations, and distribution build-out requirements that software simply doesn't. The conventional framework: hardware seed-stage companies (pre-revenue, proof of concept) typically raise at ₹10–30 crore valuations. ₹133 crore is a Series B-equivalent valuation for a company that hadn't yet shipped a single paid unit. The misalignment between business stage and valuation stage is the deal's central problem.

**Pre-Bookings vs. Revenue:** Pre-bookings (advance fees collected before product delivery) are encouraging indicators of demand intent — they show that 1,000 households were interested enough to put money down. But they are not revenue. Sharks evaluate revenue because it represents actual completed value exchanges: customer received product, evaluated it, decided it was worth the price, and paid. Pre-bookings can be cancelled. Revenue cannot be uncollected. The gap between ₹10 lakh in pre-booking fees and ₹0 in delivered-product revenue is the gap between a compelling concept and a functioning business.

**Gross Margin in Hardware vs. Software:** Chewie's ~40% gross margin is respectable for early-stage hardware but significantly below what's needed to support the aggressive scaling targets (10,000 units by FY27). As manufacturing volume increases and component costs are renegotiated, gross margins can improve toward 55–65% — but that improvement requires actually producing and selling at scale first. The journey from 40% to 60% gross margin requires capital investment in tooling, supplier relationships, and manufacturing partnerships that the ₹2 crore ask alone cannot fund.

---

### 7. Market Opportunity & What Would Change the Story

**India's Waste Management Market:**
- Urban solid waste generated annually: 62 million tonnes
- Wet/organic waste share: 50–60% (~30–37 million tonnes)
- Formal composting market: ~₹2,000–3,000 crore, growing rapidly as municipal bodies implement source segregation mandates
- Home composting appliance segment: Nascent — estimated ₹200–500 crore, high-growth potential as urban awareness increases

**TAM → SAM → SOM:**
- **TAM:** Urban Indian households generating organic waste — 70 million households
- **SAM:** SEC-A households in Tier 1 cities open to premium sustainability appliances — ~5–7 million households
- **SOM (5-year):** 50,000–100,000 units at ₹34,999 = ₹175–350 crore — achievable with proper retail distribution

**What would make Chewie fundable at a lower valuation:**
1. **500 delivered, paid units with post-sale data:** Actual compost output quality, device reliability, customer satisfaction scores — the evidence that the product works in real Indian kitchens
2. **Manufacturing cost roadmap:** Show how COGS moves from current 60% to 35–40% at 5,000 unit volume — demonstrating the path to sustainable margins
3. **Retail distribution pilot:** One agreement with a premium electronics retailer (Vijay Sales, Croma, or a D2C platform) showing the distribution channel exists
4. **Narrowed market entry:** Pick one city, one customer segment, achieve 50% market share there, then scale — instead of simultaneous national + UK ambitions

---

### 8. Takeaway for Learners

**Valuation must be anchored to evidence, not potential.** The Mankomb team is among the most credentialled in this season — IIM, 15-year corporate AI careers, patents, international experience. Their potential is not in question. Their ₹133 crore valuation was. Credentials justify confidence; they don't justify multiples. What justifies multiples is traction — revenue, users, contracts, delivery records. Come back when you have 500 delivered units and the data from those deployments.

**Hardware founders need to solve distribution before pitching scale.** "We'll sell 10,000 units in FY27" requires a distribution answer: through which stores, platforms, or channels, at what margin per channel, with what after-sales service infrastructure. Without that answer, the projection is hope, not plan.

**A great team reduces risk but doesn't replace evidence.** Investors routinely back great teams with early traction over good teams with compelling concepts. Mankomb had a great team and a compelling concept — but no traction. Adding even minimal revenue evidence (100 delivered units with 4.5-star reviews) would have changed the room's posture dramatically.

## PITCH 3 — MADRASI KAAPI HOUSE
### *Authentic South Indian Filter Coffee in Mumbai — The Hardest City to Scale a Regional Brand*

---

### 1. Business Snapshot

Madrasi Kaapi House (MKH) is a Mumbai-based Quick Service Restaurant (QSR) brand founded in 2019 by neighbours-turned-business-partners **Venkata Shiva**, **Simran Venkat**, and **Smita Rohan Alke**. It serves authentic South Indian filter coffee — not the watered-down "south Indian coffee" served at most Mumbai dhabas and Udupi restaurants — alongside complementary South Indian snacks, using a standardised, chef-less, process-driven model that enables consistent quality across outlets without skilled barista dependency.

The brand's distinctive technical capability: a single brew of filter coffee decoction can produce **30+ distinct beverage varieties** — kaapi, cold kaapi, kaapi milkshakes, hot and iced variations with different milk ratios — from one base preparation. This efficiency is not accidental; it reflects the founders' deep understanding of South Indian coffee culture and the science of decoction preparation.

---

### 2. Product & What It Solves

**The problem:** Filter coffee — the slow-brewed, decoction-based coffee prepared in the traditional South Indian percolating filter — is arguably India's most sophisticated regional coffee culture. The ritual involves specific decoction strength, precise milk-to-decoction ratios, and ideally the "pulling" technique (pouring the coffee between vessels to create froth and cool it simultaneously). The experience is completely different from espresso, drip coffee, or instant coffee. And in Mumbai — home to significant South Indian communities and a massive café culture — there was no credible, accessible, scalable version of it.

Udupi restaurants serve a version, but it's an afterthought on a meals menu. Third-wave coffee shops serve espresso with South Indian labels that don't actually taste like filter coffee. MKH was founded specifically to fill this gap: authentic filter coffee, fast, consistent, at a price point accessible to working professionals.

**The process-driven model:** MKH standardised its decoction preparation to a degree that removes barista dependency. The system is documented, trained, and quality-controlled — enabling franchisees to maintain the same product quality as owned outlets without requiring the founders' personal presence. This is the fundamental operational breakthrough that makes a food & beverage brand franchisable, and MKH achieved it early.

---

### 3. Numbers That Matter

| Financial Year | Revenue | EBITDA Margin |
|---|---|---|
| FY 2019–20 | ₹8 lakh | — |
| FY 2020–21 | ₹29.36 lakh | — |
| FY 2022–23 | ₹1.36 crore | -24% |
| FY 2023–24 | ₹1.78 crore | -24% |
| FY 2024–25 | ₹2.5 crore | -2% |
| FY 2025–26 (Projected) | ₹2.95 crore | — |

**Outlet Structure:**
- Total outlets: 5 (2 company-owned, 3 franchises)
- Channel split: 76% offline, 24% online (Swiggy/Zomato)

**Reading the EBITDA trend:** The improvement from -24% in FY23 to -2% in FY25 is meaningful — the business is approaching breakeven at current scale. But ₹2.5 crore revenue across 5 outlets means approximately ₹50 lakh per outlet annually (₹4.2 lakh per month) — modest for Mumbai locations where rent alone could be ₹1–3 lakh per month. The path from -2% to meaningful EBITDA requires either higher per-outlet revenue or more outlets spreading fixed costs — and both require the capital the founders were seeking.

**The four closed outlets — the Sharks' central concern:** MKH previously operated more outlets that were subsequently closed. This history is significant: it means the founders have direct experience of what doesn't work — location selection, unit economics, or operational management — and either learned from it or repeated the mistake. Sharks asking about this are not being cruel; they're trying to determine whether the failures produced operational wisdom or simply attrition.

---

### 4. Final Deal

**❌ No Deal — All Sharks passed**

**Anupam's concern — location selection:** Poor outlet location choices were explicitly cited. In F&B, location is not just a factor — it is the business model. A filter coffee outlet in the right Mumbai micro-market (Matunga's South Indian community, BKC's corporate office cluster, Powai's tech office parks) generates walk-in traffic and repeat purchase behaviour with zero marketing spend. The same outlet in the wrong location requires persistent, expensive customer acquisition to survive. MKH's four outlet closures suggest location selection was a recurring challenge — and without a credible framework for future location evaluation, Sharks can't model what happens to the next 10 outlets.

**Namita's concern — national scaling against Western competitors:** Scaling a regional coffee brand nationally in India means competing with Café Coffee Day (₹1,100+ crore revenue), Starbucks, Barista, and the rapidly growing Third Wave Coffee and Blue Tokai in the premium segment. Each of these has distribution advantages, brand recognition, and — for international brands — real estate negotiating leverage that a 5-outlet Mumbai startup cannot match. Namita's "how do you compete nationally" question demanded a specific, differentiated answer. A broad "filter coffee is different" response wasn't sufficient.

**The valuation challenge:** ₹75 lakh for 5% implies ₹15 crore valuation on ₹2.5 crore revenue — a 6x revenue multiple for a near-breakeven F&B QSR chain. F&B chains typically trade at 1–2x revenue at early stage because of the capital intensity and execution risk of scaling physical outlets. A 6x ask requires a compelling growth narrative and execution track record that four closed outlets and near-breakeven financials couldn't support.

---

### 5. Key Business Terms in Action

**Unit Economics in QSR:** The fundamental F&B investment metric is **per-outlet EBITDA** — how much profit each individual outlet generates before head-office allocation. If each MKH outlet generates ₹4.2 lakh monthly revenue at a 10% outlet EBITDA, that's ₹42,000 per outlet per month in profit. At 5 outlets, that's ₹2.1 lakh monthly before head-office costs. For a business requiring significant capital to scale to 50 outlets, this per-outlet EBITDA is the foundational investment case — and it needed to be presented clearly.

**Franchise vs. Company-Owned outlet economics:** MKH's 3 franchises and 2 company-owned splits carry different economics. Company-owned: higher revenue capture, higher operational control, but also full capital and operational risk. Franchise: lower revenue capture (franchise fee + royalty), reduced operational burden, but dependent on franchisee quality. In scaling F&B brands, the franchise model is attractive because it grows the footprint without proportionate capital deployment. MKH's existing franchise relationships could be the scaling mechanism — but the pitch needed to demonstrate what makes a good MKH franchisee, what the franchisee economics look like, and what the support system for franchisees is.

**Location as business model in F&B:** In quick service restaurants, location determines 60–70% of outlet success. Foot traffic patterns, proximity to target demographic, visibility, accessibility, and competitive density are all location variables. The art of F&B chain building is location selection methodology — not just finding good locations opportunistically but building a repeatable framework for evaluating and securing them systematically. MKH's four outlet closures suggest this framework wasn't fully developed. The pitch needed to present: "Here is how we evaluate locations, here is what we look for, here is why our open outlets succeeded and our closed ones failed, here is how we apply that learning."

---

### 6. Market Opportunity & What Would Change the Story

**India's Coffee Market:**
- Total: ₹32,000+ crore and growing at 11–12% CAGR
- QSR coffee segment: ₹8,000–10,000 crore
- Filter coffee specifically: Large within South India, underdeveloped nationally — estimated ₹1,000–2,000 crore formal market nationally, primarily through regional chains

**The authentic regional coffee opportunity:** Third-wave coffee in India has created a consumer class willing to pay ₹200–350 for a carefully prepared specialty coffee. The same consumer, if properly educated, would pay ₹80–150 for a perfectly prepared South Indian filter coffee — a drink with more inherent complexity and cultural depth than most espresso preparations. MKH's product, if positioned correctly and distributed in the right locations, serves this consumer well.

**TAM → SAM → SOM:**
- **TAM:** India's coffee café market — ₹32,000 crore
- **SAM:** Urban professionals in Tier 1 cities seeking authentic regional coffee alternatives to Starbucks/CCD — ~₹3,000–5,000 crore
- **SOM (5-year realistic):** ₹25–50 crore with 30–50 outlets — achievable through franchise-first expansion in South Indian community micro-markets nationally

**What would make MKH fundable:**
1. **Outlet-level EBITDA presentation:** Show each outlet's individual P&L — not just company-level revenue. If 2 of 5 outlets are profitable at 15%+ EBITDA, that's the investment case.
2. **Location selection framework:** Document what makes a successful MKH location (South Indian community concentration, office proximity, footfall threshold) and show it predicted the successes and failures
3. **Franchise economics:** Present the franchisee agreement, the support system, and a testimonial from an existing franchisee on their economics
4. **National map:** Identify the 20 South Indian community clusters across India (Matunga Mumbai, Koramangala Bangalore, Anna Nagar Chennai, Kondapur Hyderabad) where MKH's product naturally has a waiting audience

---

### 7. Takeaway for Learners

**In F&B, outlet-level economics are the pitch.** Company-level revenue growing from ₹8 lakh to ₹2.5 crore is impressive. But Sharks invest in the per-outlet model — if each outlet generates ₹X profit at ₹Y revenue with ₹Z capital investment, that's the unit that multiplies when you scale. Present the individual outlet P&L first, company P&L second.

**Failed outlets are not disqualifying — unexplained failed outlets are.** Four outlet closures are not a dealbreaker if the founders can articulate precisely why each failed, what they learned, and how that learning is embedded in current operations. "We chose bad locations, here's our new location scoring methodology, here's how it would have prevented those failures" is a compelling answer. Silence or vague attribution to "COVID" is not.

**Regional cultural products have natural audiences — map them before pitching national scale.** MKH doesn't need to compete with Starbucks nationally. It needs to find the 20 South Indian community micro-markets across India where its product has a waiting, culturally familiar audience. That map — specific neighbourhoods, populations, existing F&B spend data — is the national scaling strategy. Present it instead of claiming "we'll go national."

**The chef-less, process-driven model is genuinely valuable — it just needed better presentation.** Standardising South Indian filter coffee to a reproducible, franchisable process is a real operational achievement that most regional food brands never accomplish. This is MKH's actual moat — not the product (which competitors could attempt to replicate), but the documented, trainable system that produces consistent product without artisanal dependency. Lead with that moat in the pitch.

## EPISODE SUMMARY — PERSONAL CONVICTION AND COMMERCIAL ARCHITECTURE

Episode 24 presents three businesses with authentic, deeply personal founding stories. All three are building in genuinely large markets. All three have founders who clearly believe in what they're creating. Only one walked out with a deal.

**Wholeleaf succeeded** because Shivraj did the hardest work before the pitch: 13 clinical trials, government licensing, Apollo Pharmacy relationships, and 16.5x growth in 18 months. His personal conviction was converted into commercial evidence before he asked anyone for money. The Sharks weren't investing in his story — they were investing in proof.

**Chewie didn't secure a deal** because the team's exceptional credentials and genuine technical innovation were priced at a valuation that required evidence they hadn't yet generated. The business needed 6–12 more months of delivered units, customer data, and manufacturing cost validation before the ₹133 crore number could be defended. This is not a business failure — it's a timing and positioning failure. Come back with traction.

**Madrasi Kaapi House didn't secure a deal** because the founders couldn't fully articulate what they had learned from four outlet closures, couldn't present per-outlet unit economics clearly, and couldn't map a credible national scaling path against formidable incumbents. The product is excellent; the business architecture wasn't fully assembled for a Shark Tank pitch.

**The episode's meta-lesson:** Great businesses need two things — a founder who lived the problem, and an architecture that turns that experience into scalable, repeatable value creation. Personal conviction is the starting point. Commercial architecture is what investors fund. The distance between the two is where most pitches fail.
