# Shark Tank India Season 5 — Episode 10: Driven By Innovation

**Air Date:** January 20, 2026

**Sharks Panel:** Namita Thapar, Kunal Bahl, Viraj Bahl, Varun Alagh, Vineeta Singh

**Episode Success Rate:** 2 of 3 deals closed (67%)

Three businesses walked in with very different answers to the same question: how do you build something people actually need? RidEV leased electric scooters to gig workers who couldn't afford to buy one. Stroom packed protein into centre-filled bars for people who skipped breakfast. TBFO built a crore-rupee fashion brand entirely through Instagram Lives — zero ad spend. The episode rewarded the first two and taught the third a sharp lesson about what "impressive" means versus what "investable" means.

---

## PITCH 1: RidEV

### Business Snapshot

RidEV is an electric two-wheeler leasing platform that solves a brutal economic catch-22 facing India's gig delivery workers: they need electric scooters to earn, but can't afford to buy one. RidEV purchases branded e-scooters in bulk, then leases them to human resource companies who deploy them to last-mile delivery personnel working for quick commerce platforms — Zepto, Blinkit, Swiggy Instamart, and others.

The model is asset-heavy (the company owns the vehicles) but operationally lean (HR companies manage the rider relationships). RidEV sits in the middle — owning the iron, collecting the lease income, and bearing the depreciation and maintenance risk. What makes it work: the economics of quick commerce delivery demand reliable, cost-efficient vehicles, and electric scooters beat petrol bikes on fuel cost by a wide margin once the upfront purchase barrier is removed.

The parent company is **ANV Web Ventures Pvt. Ltd.**, registered in Hyderabad and now operational across six cities.

### Founders

**Manish Kumar Jain** — Chartered Accountant by training, serial entrepreneur by instinct. His CA background gave him sharp financial modelling skills essential for an asset-leasing business where unit economics per vehicle determine everything. He founded ANV Web Ventures and brought the structured thinking needed to build a capital-intensive business from scratch.

**Siddharth Jain** — Manish's cousin, co-founder handling operations and expansion. The cousin duo structure meant both founders had skin in the game through family trust — Sharks noted this dynamic positively, seeing it as alignment without the awkwardness family businesses sometimes carry.

Kunal Bahl flagged that Manish had attempted several businesses before RidEV — some unsuccessful. Rather than penalising this, Kunal treated it as data: founders who survive multiple failures develop judgment that first-timers can't. The pattern across S5 is consistent — Sharks increasingly reward resilience over clean track records.

### Numbers That Matter

| Metric | Figure |
|---|---|
| Fleet size | 4,000+ electric two-wheelers |
| Deployment timeline | 4,000 units in 6 months |
| Cities operational | 6 — Hyderabad, Mumbai, Chennai, Delhi, Pune, Bangalore |
| Revenue model | Monthly lease income per vehicle |
| Deployment partners | Quick commerce HR companies |
| End customers | Zepto, Blinkit, Swiggy Instamart delivery networks |
| Ask | ₹6 crore for 3% equity (₹200 crore valuation) |

The 4,000-unit fleet in six months is the headline. Each vehicle generates steady monthly lease revenue — the exact per-unit figure wasn't disclosed publicly, but at typical EV lease rates of ₹8,000–12,000/month, the fleet runs a ₹3.2–4.8 crore monthly revenue engine. That's a substantial number for a business barely a year old.

### Deal Outcome: DEAL SECURED — KUNAL BAHL (Solo) ✅

**Final Terms:** Kunal Bahl invested solo, backing the long-term potential of India's EV mobility sector and the founders' execution track record. Exact final equity percentage and valuation adjustment not fully disclosed in sources, but the deal reflected "confidence in mobility as a future-facing category, while acknowledging the capital-intensive nature of the business."

Kunal investing solo — rather than syndicating — signals he saw this as a sector bet requiring a single decisive investor with conviction, not a consortium hedging uncertainty.

### Key Business Terms

**Asset-Based Leasing Model:** RidEV buys vehicles outright, then leases them monthly. Revenue is predictable (contracted lease payments), but capital deployment is front-loaded and heavy. The company essentially becomes a financing intermediary — converting a ₹1–1.5 lakh purchase decision into a ₹8,000–12,000/month operating cost for riders. This is the same logic behind car leasing, but applied to last-mile delivery.

**Unit Economics Per Vehicle:** The critical metric for any fleet business. Each scooter costs ₹80,000–1,50,000 to purchase. Monthly lease revenue is ₹8,000–12,000. Maintenance, insurance, and depreciation eat into that. The payback period per vehicle (how many months of lease income to recoup purchase cost) determines whether the business creates or destroys value. Sharks drilled into this number — it's the heartbeat of RidEV's entire model.

**End-of-Life Risk:** A pointed question during the pitch. Electric two-wheelers in India have limited resale markets — unlike petrol bikes which hold value. When a battery degrades after 3–5 years, what's the scooter worth? RidEV must factor this depreciation into unit economics, or the model breaks down at scale. This was one of the "heated debates" the episode featured.

**Quick Commerce Tailwind:** India's quick commerce market (Zepto, Blinkit, Swiggy Instamart) is growing 40–50% annually, directly driving demand for delivery riders and therefore delivery vehicles. RidEV's fate is structurally tied to this sector's growth — a tailwind if quick commerce scales, a headwind if it consolidates or contracts.

### Market Context

India's electric two-wheeler market sold approximately 2.2 million units in 2024, growing toward a projected 10–22 million units annually by 2030. The segment is one of the fastest-growing in Indian mobility, driven by government subsidies (PM e-DRIVE scheme), falling battery costs, and the economics of fuel savings.

The gig delivery economy adds a specific layer: an estimated 5–8 million delivery riders operate in India, most renting or owning petrol two-wheelers. The transition to electric creates a leasing opportunity because riders are cost-sensitive (they won't spend ₹1.5 lakh upfront) but will happily pay a monthly lease if it's cheaper than their current fuel bill.

**Competitive Landscape:**
- **Bounce, Ola Electric, Hero Electric:** Manufacturers, not leasing platforms — they sell vehicles, not access
- **Revamp Moto** (S1 Shark Tank alumni): Modular EV platform, different use case (utility attachments)
- **BGauss, Kabira:** Leasing-adjacent models but focused on consumer purchase, not fleet deployment
- **RidEV's edge:** Pure-play leasing platform purpose-built for quick commerce last-mile — no direct equivalent at scale in India

### Why Kunal Invested Alone

Kunal's solo bet reflects a specific investment thesis: EV mobility infrastructure is a decade-long structural shift, not a quarterly revenue play. The capital intensity scared off other Sharks — deploying 4,000 scooters costs ₹40–60 crore, and scaling to 40,000 costs ten times that. Most Sharks prefer asset-light models. Kunal saw the other side: whoever builds the largest EV leasing fleet for gig delivery first creates a defensible position competitors can't easily replicate. Fleet size is the moat.

### Takeaways

**Asset-heavy businesses need patient capital.** RidEV's model requires buying thousands of scooters before revenue flows. This isn't a software business where you ship and iterate — it's infrastructure. Founders in capital-intensive sectors must find investors comfortable with slower payback and longer horizons.

**Fleet size is the moat.** Once RidEV has 40,000 scooters deployed, a competitor needs ₹400+ crore and years of execution to match. Scale creates defensibility in ways brand or technology alone can't in fleet businesses.

**End-of-life planning is non-negotiable.** The Sharks' scrutiny of battery depreciation and resale value wasn't hypothetical — it's the difference between a profitable fleet and a money pit. EV leasing companies that don't solve the end-of-life problem will destroy value as batteries age.

## PITCH 2: STROOM

### Business Snapshot

Stroom is a protein snack brand built around a single product innovation: **Asia's first centre-filled energy and protein bars.** The format matters — conventional protein bars are dry, chalky, and taste like punishment. Stroom's centre-filled design (nut butter core wrapped in a flavoured outer layer) delivers texture and taste that makes protein consumption feel like indulgence rather than obligation.

The product line has two pillars: **milk protein bars** (centre-filled, 7–20g protein per bar depending on SKU) and **plant protein wafers** (lighter, crunchier alternative). Both are sweetened with honey, contain no added sugar, and are gluten-free and non-GMO. Pricing sits at ₹50–75 per bar — accessible enough for daily consumption, not so cheap as to signal low quality.

The brand's origin story includes a small but memorable detail: the founders once ran into Vineeta Singh at D-Mart, offered her their coffee-flavoured bar, and she genuinely enjoyed it. When Vineeta later appeared as a Shark on their pitch, that encounter added a human thread to an otherwise straightforward consumer pitch.

### Founders

Details on Stroom's founding team were limited in public sources. What emerged from the pitch: the founders identified breakfast as the most skipped meal in urban India and saw protein bars as the fastest-access solution. The brand's mission statement — "make good health accessible to all" — positions Stroom as democratizing nutrition rather than catering to gym bros willing to pay ₹200 per bar.

The centre-filled format required R&D investment and manufacturing partnership — not trivial to execute at scale. The fact that Stroom achieved retail presence (Amazon, Flipkart, and importantly D-Mart) suggests operational capability beyond a kitchen-table startup.

### Numbers That Matter

| Metric | Figure |
|---|---|
| Product innovation | Asia's first centre-filled protein/energy bar |
| Protein content | 7g (standard bars) to 20g (premium bars) |
| Flavours | Dark Chocolate, Milk Chocolate, Creamy Peanut Butter, Coffee, Choco Almond |
| Price range | ₹50–75 per bar (accessible tier) |
| Distribution | Amazon, Flipkart, D-Mart, own website |
| Sweetener | Honey (no added sugar) |
| Dietary positioning | Gluten-free, non-GMO, no artificial additives |
| Ask | ₹1 crore for 2% equity (₹50 crore valuation) |

Specific revenue figures were not publicly disclosed. However, D-Mart presence is a meaningful signal — D-Mart is notoriously selective about new brands, requiring consistent demand proof before shelf allocation. Getting listed there validates retail-readiness and consumer pull beyond online-only brands.

### Deal Outcome: DEAL SECURED — KUNAL BAHL + VINEETA SINGH ✅

**Final Terms:** Kunal Bahl and Vineeta Singh closed the deal. Per LocalSamosa's week-2 roundup, "The Sharks focused on unit economics, customer retention, and expansion potential. Kunal Bahl and Vineeta Singh closed the deal, indicating confidence in the business model and the founders' execution capability."

The two-Shark structure makes strategic sense: Kunal brings consumer brand scaling expertise (Snapdeal's marketplace taught him what products move at volume), and Vineeta understands D2C consumer brands intimately from SUGAR Cosmetics. Together they cover distribution strategy and brand building — the two levers that convert a good product into a large business.

### Key Business Terms

**Centre-Filled Format Innovation:** Most protein bars use a single extruded layer — cheap to manufacture, easy to scale, but texturally boring. Centre-filling requires a more complex manufacturing process (depositing nut butter core, then enrobing with outer layer) but creates a product experience competitors can't match by simply tweaking recipes. This is "product innovation as moat" — the format itself becomes defensible.

**Accessible Protein Positioning:** At ₹50–75/bar, Stroom targets the mass market rather than the fitness enthusiast niche (where ₹200+ bars from MuscleBlaze or Optimum Nutrition dominate). This pricing opens the TAM dramatically — every working professional who skips breakfast is a potential customer, not just gym-goers. The trade-off: thinner margins per unit, requiring higher volume to reach venture scale.

**D-Mart as Distribution Signal:** D-Mart (DMart) is India's largest value retail chain with 350+ stores. Unlike supermarkets that accept any brand willing to pay for shelf space, D-Mart's procurement team only stocks products with proven consumer demand. Getting listed there is third-party market validation — a signal Sharks weigh heavily when evaluating consumer brands.

**Repeat Purchase Economics:** Protein bars are consumables — once a customer finds one they like, they buy again and again. Unlike fashion or electronics (one-time purchases), consumables create natural recurring revenue. The key question for Sharks: what's the repeat rate? A customer who buys Stroom every week generates ₹2,600/year in revenue — very different economics from a one-time purchase brand.

### Market Context

India's protein supplement market exceeded ₹2,500 crore in 2024 and is growing 15–20% annually. Protein bars specifically are the fastest-growing subcategory, driven by rising fitness awareness, busy urban lifestyles, and the shift from "gym supplement" to "everyday nutrition" perception.

**Competitive Landscape:**
- **High-protein tier (₹150–250/bar):** MuscleBlaze, Optimum Nutrition, MyProtein — gym-focused, high protein (25–30g), premium positioning
- **Mid-tier (₹75–150/bar):** TBruce, Protein Desis, various D2C brands — growing fast, mixed quality
- **Accessible tier (₹40–75/bar):** Where Stroom operates — limited competition, massive volume opportunity
- **Adjacent:** Protein wafers (newer format), protein cookies, protein shakes — category expanding rapidly

**Stroom's differentiation:** Centre-filled format + accessible pricing + clean ingredients = unique position at the intersection of taste, health, and affordability. No direct competitor occupies this exact space.

### Why Kunal and Vineeta Invested

**Kunal** saw a scalable consumer brand with product-level differentiation. The centre-filled format isn't something a competitor can copy overnight — manufacturing capability, recipe development, and supply chain for nut butter filling all take time. Combined with D-Mart presence proving retail readiness, Kunal saw a brand with genuine distribution potential.

**Vineeta** brought personal validation (the D-Mart encounter) and strategic expertise. Having scaled SUGAR Cosmetics from D2C to omnichannel, she understood exactly how to take a product that works online and build it into a national brand through retail partnerships, packaging optimization, and marketing at scale.

### Takeaways

**Format innovation creates defensibility in commoditized categories.** Protein bars are crowded, but centre-filling changed the product experience enough to matter. Founders should look for format innovations — not just ingredient tweaks — when entering competitive FMCG categories.

**Price point determines market size.** At ₹50–75, Stroom addresses every working Indian, not just fitness enthusiasts. Accessible pricing sacrifices per-unit margin but multiplies addressable customers by 10x+. The math often favors volume.

**Retail presence validates more than reviews do.** D-Mart listing is a stronger signal than 500 five-star Amazon reviews. Retailers have skin in the game — shelf space costs money, and they only allocate it to products that sell. If a Shark asks "prove demand exists," a D-Mart listing is one of the most credible answers.

**The D-Mart anecdote closed the deal.** Vineeta's genuine enjoyment of the coffee bar at D-Mart — an unscripted, organic moment — created personal investment in the brand's success. Founders should never underestimate chance encounters with future investors. Every interaction is a potential pitch.

## PITCH 3: TBFO (The Branded Factory Outlet)

### Business Snapshot

TBFO — short for **The Branded Factory Outlet** — is a women's fashion brand selling premium long dresses and co-ords for Indian women aged 25–35. What makes TBFO remarkable isn't the product (dresses are one of India's most crowded fashion categories) but the **distribution model**: the entire business runs on Instagram Lives and Reels with **zero performance marketing spend.** No Facebook ads, no Google campaigns, no influencer budget. Yashica's personality and content created the demand; Instagram's algorithm delivered the customers.

The brand started when Yashica began posting try-on videos of dresses she ordered from Jabong. The content gained traction organically. She met Avnish, they married, and together built TBFO into a brand where Instagram Lives function as real-time showrooms — customers watch Yashica model dresses live, ask questions, and buy instantly.

The pitch itself was delivered in reel format — Avnish entered with a Vodafone Ju-Ju mascot on his shoulder, Yashica presented with her signature high-energy personality. It was the most entertainment-forward pitch of the episode, and the Sharks were visibly entertained. But entertainment and investability are different currencies.

### Founders

**Yashica Gyanchandani** — Engineering graduate from Bhopal who pivoted entirely to fashion content creation and brand building. Her Instagram presence (358K+ followers on @t_b_f_o) is the brand's entire marketing engine. She's been featured in Forbes and has spoken at TEDx — credibility signals beyond Shark Tank. Her energy, expressiveness, and comfort on camera are genuine competitive advantages that can't be hired or outsourced.

**Avnish Sharma** — Yashica's husband and co-founder, handling operations, inventory, and business strategy. The couple-entrepreneur dynamic here is different from the father-son pitches earlier in S5: both founders are in the same generation, same life stage, and both deeply committed. Avnish's role as the operational backbone allowed Yashica to focus entirely on being the brand's face.

### Numbers That Matter

| Metric | Figure |
|---|---|
| Brand name | TBFO (The Branded Factory Outlet) |
| Target demographic | Women, 25–35 years |
| Product focus | Premium long dresses, co-ords |
| Instagram followers | 358K+ (@t_b_f_o) |
| Marketing spend | ₹0 (zero performance marketing) |
| Sales channel | Instagram Lives and Reels exclusively |
| Origin city | Bhopal, Madhya Pradesh |
| Ask | ₹1 crore for 5% equity (₹20 crore valuation) |
| Credibility | Forbes featured, TEDx speaker |

The zero-spend marketing model is the pitch's core claim. If true at meaningful revenue scale, it implies extraordinarily low customer acquisition costs — potentially the best unit economics in the episode. Year-on-year growth impressed Sharks, though exact revenue figures weren't publicly disclosed.

### Deal Outcome: NO DEAL ❌

Despite genuine admiration for the organic growth model and Yashica's personality, all Sharks declined. Per LocalSamosa: "The Sharks raised concerns around scalability, repeatability, and brand defensibility in a crowded fashion market. Despite appreciation for the aesthetic and intent, the pitch did not result in a deal due to concerns around long-term growth and operational scale."

### Key Business Terms

**Live Commerce:** Selling products during live-streamed video events where viewers can interact, ask questions, and purchase in real time. TBFO's Instagram Lives function as virtual showrooms — Yashica models dresses, answers questions about fit and fabric, and viewers buy directly. Live commerce is exploding in China (₹30+ lakh crore market) and gaining traction in India, but adoption remains early-stage.

**Zero Performance Marketing (Organic-Only Growth):** TBFO spends nothing on paid acquisition — no Meta ads, no Google campaigns. All customers discover the brand through Instagram's organic algorithm (Reels, Live notifications, shares). This is extraordinary if sustained at scale, but it creates a structural vulnerability: Instagram's algorithm is not under TBFO's control. A policy change, algorithm shift, or account suspension could destroy the brand's reach overnight.

**Platform Dependency (Instagram):** TBFO's entire distribution runs through a single platform owned by Meta. This is the same structural risk that killed VKYD's pitch (Roblox dependency) — when your business lives entirely inside someone else's ecosystem, you don't control your own destiny. Sharks flagged this directly.

**Founder-as-Brand:** Yashica IS the brand. Her personality, energy, and on-camera presence drive customer loyalty. This creates a personal moat (no competitor can replicate Yashica) but also a scaling ceiling — the business can only grow as fast as Yashica can personally create content and host Lives. Hiring another host dilutes the authenticity that built the brand.

### Market Context

India's women's fashion market exceeds ₹3 lakh crore, with online women's fashion growing 25–30% annually. Instagram-driven fashion commerce is a real and growing channel — brands like Angrakhaa, and dozens of smaller Instagram-native labels prove the model works at small scale.

But "works at small scale" and "scales to venture returns" are different propositions. The crowded nature of Indian women's fashion (thousands of Instagram sellers, established players like Myntra, Flipkart Fashion, and regional brands) means differentiation erodes quickly. What makes TBFO special today — Yashica's organic reach — doesn't create the kind of structural advantage that compounds over years.

### Why All Sharks Passed

The rejection wasn't about quality or founder capability. Sharks explicitly praised the zero-spend model and Yashica's energy. The concerns were structural:

**Scalability ceiling:** Yashica can only host so many Lives per week. Each Live reaches a finite audience. Growing revenue 10x requires either 10x more Lives (impossible) or paid acquisition (destroys the "zero spend" thesis) or platform expansion (risky, dilutes brand identity).

**Platform dependency:** Instagram owns the distribution. If Meta changes algorithm priorities, restricts commerce features, or suspends the account for any reason, TBFO has no fallback. No email list, no owned customer database, no retail presence — just an Instagram handle.

**Fashion category defensibility:** Dresses are endlessly replicable. Another founder with similar energy could launch a competing Instagram brand tomorrow. The product itself (long dresses, co-ords) has zero proprietary advantage — it's the distribution (Instagram organic reach) that's special, and that's algorithm-dependent, not founder-controlled.

**Founder dependency:** The business cannot function without Yashica personally. This is a lifestyle business with one irreplaceable person at its center — the opposite of what venture investors need (a business that scales independent of any single individual).

### Takeaways

**Organic growth is impressive but not sufficient.** Zero marketing spend sounds like a superpower. But if the only way to maintain it is the founder personally creating content every day on a single platform, it's a lifestyle business, not a venture business. True scalability requires owned distribution — email lists, retail partnerships, or platforms the company controls.

**Founder-as-brand is a double-edged sword.** Yashica's personality is TBFO's greatest asset and greatest vulnerability simultaneously. It created the brand from nothing; it also caps how large the brand can grow. Founders building around personal charisma must eventually build systems that don't require their constant presence.

**Instagram is distribution, not a business.** TBFO proved Instagram can build a fashion brand with zero ad spend. But Instagram is a channel, not a company. The moment Meta changes the rules, the business changes with it. Founders must diversify distribution before seeking venture capital — single-platform businesses face an automatic ceiling.

**Sharks reward systems over personalities.** Yashica's energy won hearts in the Tank. But Sharks invest in businesses that can run without the founder being the only point of failure. The episode's funded businesses (RidEV, Stroom) both had structural models — fleet economics, retail distribution — that didn't depend on one person's daily output.

## EPISODE-LEVEL INSIGHTS

### What "Driven By Innovation" Actually Meant

Each pitch innovated differently — and the results tracked exactly with how investable each innovation was:

| Brand | Innovation Type | Investable? | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **RidEV** | Structural (leasing model for EVs) | ✅ Yes | Creates fleet-scale moat; solves real affordability barrier |
| **Stroom** | Product (centre-filled format) | ✅ Yes | Replicable? No — manufacturing complexity + taste. Defensible at scale |
| **TBFO** | Distribution (Instagram-only commerce) | ❌ No | Platform-dependent; founder-dependent; no structural advantage |

The lesson: **innovations that create structural advantages at scale get funded. Innovations that depend on a single platform or single person don't.**

### The Two Deals, One Common Thread

Both RidEV and Stroom secured funding for a shared reason: **they solved problems at a scale that required capital to maintain.** RidEV needed money to buy more scooters. Stroom needed money to expand manufacturing and retail presence. Capital was the fuel for growth, not the substitute for it.

TBFO didn't need capital — it was already growing organically. But that's precisely why it wasn't investable. Venture capital funds businesses that can't grow without it and will grow dramatically with it. A business that works fine without money, but can't use money to accelerate, isn't a venture bet.

### Kunal Bahl's Episode

Kunal was the most active investor this episode — backing both RidEV (solo) and Stroom (with Vineeta). His investment logic across both reveals a consistent thesis:

- **RidEV:** Sector conviction. EV mobility is a structural shift. Whoever builds fleet infrastructure first wins. Capital intensity is a feature, not a bug — it raises the barrier to entry.
- **Stroom:** Brand conviction. Product innovation + accessible pricing + retail presence = the ingredients of a scalable consumer brand. His Snapdeal background taught him what products move at volume.

Both bets are on **businesses where scale creates defensibility** — the opposite of TBFO, where scale is the problem.

### Valuation Reality Check

| Brand | Ask Valuation | Outcome | Signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| RidEV | ₹200 Cr | Deal (terms adjusted) | Fleet businesses command premium for asset backing |
| Stroom | ₹50 Cr | Deal closed | Reasonable for consumer brand with retail traction |
| TBFO | ₹20 Cr | No Deal | Lowest ask, still rejected — valuation wasn't the issue |

TBFO's rejection at the episode's lowest valuation is the sharpest signal. When Sharks say no at ₹20 crore, they're not negotiating on price — they're saying the business model doesn't fit their investment thesis regardless of valuation. Founders should read this clearly: sometimes the answer isn't "come back with better numbers." It's "this isn't the right capital source for what you're building."

### Post-Episode Trajectories

**RidEV** will deploy Kunal's capital to expand fleet size — likely targeting 10,000+ scooters across Tier 1 and Tier 2 cities within 12 months. The quick commerce tailwind (Zepto, Blinkit growing rapidly) provides demand. The end-of-life risk remains the key variable to watch.

**Stroom** will use Kunal and Vineeta's capital and networks to accelerate retail expansion beyond D-Mart into modern trade (Reliance Retail, Spencer's) and potentially quick commerce (Blinkit, Zepto). The centre-filled format gives them a product story that works at the shelf — critical for retail success where consumers make split-second decisions.

**TBFO** continues as what it already is: a profitable, Instagram-native fashion brand with a charismatic founder at its center. The Shark Tank visibility likely boosted follower count and sales in the short term — the "as seen on Shark Tank" effect. But the business's fundamental structure remains unchanged. Yashica's next move, if she wants venture scale, is building owned distribution (email list, own app, retail partnerships) before fundraising again.
