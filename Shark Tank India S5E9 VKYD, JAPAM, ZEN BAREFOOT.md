# Shark Tank India Season 5 — Episode 9: Fashion, Faith And Feet

**Air Date:** January 15, 2026
**Sharks Panel:** Namita Thapar, Kunal Bahl, Viraj Bahl, Varun Alagh, Vineeta Singh
**Episode Success Rate:** 1 of 3 deals closed (33%)

The episode's title did the heavy lifting — each pitch mapped cleanly onto one word. VKYD brought **Fashion** into the metaverse. Japam carried **Faith** into everyday wearables. Zen Barefoot asked us to rethink how we walk — literally. Three radically different businesses, one common thread: founders betting that familiar categories could be reinvented from the ground up.

## PITCH 1: VKYD (Meta Fashion)

### Business Snapshot

VKYD, operating under parent company **Meta Fashion**, is India's first phygital (physical + digital) fashion brand. The concept is straightforward but the execution is ahead of its time: customers buy virtual outfits for their avatars inside games like Roblox, then order the same designs as real-world clothing delivered to their doorstep. The brand bridges two worlds — gaming culture and physical fashion — under one roof.

Arjun built two platforms inside Meta Fashion: **VKYD** (the flagship brand) and **GlamGirls** (a collaboration with YouTuber Leah Ashe). Both sell virtual avatar outfits on Roblox and simultaneously offer the physical versions as made-to-order garments crafted by Indian artisans.

The business model also solves a structural inefficiency: US fashion buyers routinely overpay for lower-quality Indian goods because of middlemen. VKYD bypasses that entirely — premium Indian manufacturing, zero markup, direct to consumer.

### Founders

**Arjun Goel** — 18 years old at the time of pitch, currently a Class 12 student at Sanskriti School, New Delhi. During COVID lockdowns, Arjun became obsessed with Roblox and started designing virtual clothing for avatars. What started as a hobby turned into a revenue-generating business. Within three years, he had designed for brands including **Monstercat, Walmart, and Roblox itself** — remarkable client logos for someone still in school.

**Sanjay Goel** (Father) — Co-founder, described by Kunal Bahl as a "serial entrepreneur." Sanjay has attempted multiple businesses across his career — not all successful — which gave him operational and business-side depth Arjun lacked at 18. The father-son dynamic mirrors several other S5 pitches: younger founder with the vision, older founder with the execution instincts.

### Numbers That Matter

| Metric | Figure |
|---|---|
| Cumulative digital clothing sales | ~₹2 crore |
| Platform | Roblox (global, 70M+ daily active users) |
| Sub-brands | VKYD + GlamGirls |
| Brand collaborations | Monstercat, Walmart, Roblox |
| Business model | Made-to-order physical + digital bundle |
| Ask | ₹1 crore for 10% equity (₹10 crore valuation) |

Revenue is generated on two axes: virtual outfits sold inside Roblox (high margin, no inventory) and physical garments manufactured on demand. The ₹2 crore cumulative figure for digital sales alone — earned by a teenager — was the pitch's headline number.

### Deal Outcome: NO DEAL

Despite admiration for Arjun's age, hustle, and early traction, no Shark invested. The core tension: the business is impressive as a proof-of-concept and a founder story, but its scalability and defensibility within the Shark Tank investment horizon remain unclear. Roblox's platform policies could shift, physical fashion margins are thin, and the phygital category is still nascent with no clear path to ₹100 crore+ revenue.

### Key Business Terms

**Phygital:** A portmanteau of "physical" and "digital" describing products or experiences that exist in both worlds simultaneously. VKYD's model lets customers buy a virtual skin for their game avatar and receive the identical garment physically — collapsing the gap between online identity and real-world wardrobe.

**Made-to-Order (MTO):** Products manufactured only after a customer places an order — zero unsold inventory. This is critical for a fashion brand with niche, limited-edition designs. MTO eliminates waste and holding costs but requires flexible manufacturing partnerships and slightly longer delivery timelines.

**Platform Dependency Risk:** VKYD's digital revenue flows entirely through Roblox. If Roblox changes creator monetization policies, restricts third-party brands, or loses user base, the business's digital arm shrinks overnight. This single-platform exposure is a structural vulnerability Sharks weigh heavily.

### Market Context

Roblox reported over **70 million daily active users** globally, with virtual goods generating billions in revenue annually. India's gaming market is valued at ₹10,000+ crore and growing 20%+ year-on-year. Virtual fashion — digital clothing for avatars — is an emerging subcategory within this ecosystem, still early but directionally significant.

The physical fashion side competes in India's massive ₹6+ lakh crore apparel market, but VKYD targets a highly specific niche: gaming-native consumers who want their online identity reflected in real life. This audience is young (13–25), digitally fluent, and willing to pay for exclusivity — but it's also small today and its purchasing power for premium physical fashion is limited.

### Why Sharks Passed

The Sharks were genuinely impressed — Kunal called Sanjay a serial entrepreneur with clear admiration, and all five acknowledged Arjun's remarkable initiative at 18. But investment decisions aren't applause votes. Key concerns: Roblox platform dependency, unclear unit economics on physical garments, the category's immaturity, and whether a teenager can sustain execution at venture scale. The Sharks respected the journey; they just couldn't underwrite the risk at this stage.

### Takeaways

**Platform risk is an existential question.** Vryse (E7) faced identical scrutiny around AI platform dependency. Businesses built on top of someone else's ecosystem — Roblox, Google, Amazon — must articulate how they survive if that platform changes the rules. VKYD didn't have that answer convincingly.

**Age is not a disqualifier — but it's not a substitute for traction either.** Arjun's story was the episode's most inspiring. But ₹2 crore in cumulative digital sales, while impressive for an 18-year-old, doesn't yet demonstrate the repeatable, scalable revenue engine Sharks need to write a check.

**Father-son pitches are becoming a pattern.** Zen Barefoot and VKYD both featured father-son duos. The dynamic works when roles are clearly divided — vision versus operations — but Sharks watch for whether the younger founder can stand independently.

## PITCH 2: JAPAM

### Business Snapshot

Japam is a D2C spiritual wearables brand that sells **authentic Rudraksha beads, energy stones, spiritual gems, and Karungali wood products** — reimagined for modern, urban Indian consumers. Founded in December 2022 by serial entrepreneur Ritoban Chakrabarti out of Mohali, Punjab, the brand's core mission is brutally simple: in a market flooded with fake Rudraksha, Japam sells only the real thing — and proves it.

The brand's product range has expanded from initial Rudraksha bracelets and malas to gold-plated jewelry, idols, wall hangings, puja accessories, and gift hampers. Every product is designed to be wearable at the office — not just at the temple. This "spirituality as everyday style" positioning is what separates Japam from traditional spiritual product sellers.

Japam's quality assurance is built around a proprietary system called **BTR (Batch Test Reports):** every batch of Rudraksha is randomly selected, lab-tested for authenticity, and the report ships with the order. Customers can scan a QR code to verify. In a market where 80–90% of Rudraksha sold online is fake, this transparency became Japam's sharpest weapon.

### Founder

**Ritoban Chakrabarti** is not a first-time entrepreneur. His journey spans 19 years of business-building:

- Built nearly **400 micro-websites** after college, many ranking globally — teaching himself SEO, programming, and affiliate marketing from scratch
- Launched **Dekho.com**, one of India's earliest SaaS platforms
- Created **Flying Start Online**, one of India's top e-commerce learning platforms with a YouTube channel running free 30-day live business challenges
- Launched **Common Panda** in the US market

Japam emerged from a deeper personal pull — toward Indian heritage and spirituality — rather than pure commercial instinct. Ritoban told Indian Startup Times: he wanted to "follow Indian philosophy" and saw an opportunity where trust was the rarest commodity.

His entrance into the Tank — all-ethnic attire, stones in hand, reciting a verse from Shiv Purana about Mahadev — was theatrical but earned. It set the tone before a single business number was shared. Varun Alagh called him "real CEO material" during the pitch.

### Numbers That Matter

| Metric | Figure |
|---|---|
| Founded | December 2022 |
| FY23 Revenue | ₹85 lakh |
| ARR (at time of pitch) | ₹30+ crore (claimed); ₹60 crore projected for FY25-26 |
| Customers served (lifetime) | 3 lakh+ (1 million+ per brand's own claim) |
| Monthly customers | 35,000 |
| Daily orders (current) | ~1,200 |
| Average order value | ₹700 |
| Social media ad reach | 5 crore people/month |
| Micro-influencer network | Active, education-focused content creators |
| Ask | ₹1.5 crore for 1% equity (₹150 crore valuation) |

The revenue trajectory is steep: ₹85 lakh in FY23 to ₹30+ crore ARR within two years. Daily orders grew from 200 (Q1 2023) to 1,200 at pitch time — a 6x operational scaling in under 24 months.

### Deal Outcome: ₹1.5 CRORE FROM NAMITA THAPAR + VARUN ALAGH ✅

**Final Terms:** ₹1.5 crore for 1% equity + 1% royalty (payable until invested capital is fully recouped)

The deal went through at the **original ask valuation of ₹150 crore** — rare in Shark Tank, where haircuts are the norm. The royalty clause added downside protection for the Sharks: at ₹30+ crore annual revenue, 1% royalty returns ₹30+ lakh per year, meaning the ₹1.5 crore investment pays itself back in ~5 years through cash flow alone — independent of any exit.

**Notable tension:** Viraj Bahl stepped out of negotiations over ethical concerns about commercializing faith. Kunal Bahl pushed back on this, defending Japam's legitimacy. The debate illustrated how spiritual commerce occupies uncomfortable territory for some investors — profitable market, genuine cultural demand, but uneasy optics.

### Key Business Terms

**BTR (Batch Test Reports):** Japam's proprietary quality system. Rudraksha beads are divided into random batches, each batch lab-tested for authenticity, and the lab report accompanies every order. This systematic verification process transforms a trust problem into a transparency solution — the single most important operational innovation in the pitch.

**Micro-Influencer Strategy:** Rather than paying celebrity endorsements, Japam works with smaller creators who produce educational, research-backed content about Rudraksha and spirituality. These creators have tighter audience relationships and higher engagement rates than macro-influencers. The content itself drives organic discovery — people searching "is my Rudraksha real" land on Japam's ecosystem.

**Royalty Clause:** The 1% royalty until capital recovery acts as a cash-flow safety net for investors. If Japam never achieves a traditional exit (IPO or acquisition), Sharks still recoup their money through annual royalty payments. This structure suits businesses with strong revenue but uncertain exit timelines — exactly Japam's profile as a spiritual commerce brand.

**Festival-Driven Product Launches:** Japam creates limited-edition products timed to festivals — "Rudraksha Ki Rakhi" for Raksha Bandhan being a standout example. This converts cultural occasions into commerce moments, drives media coverage, and keeps the product catalog fresh without diluting the core brand.

### Market Context

India's religious and spiritual products market was valued at **$58.56 billion in 2023**, projected to grow at 10% CAGR through 2032. The spiritual wearables subcategory — Rudraksha, gemstones, Karungali — is a significant slice of this, served overwhelmingly by unorganized sellers with no quality controls.

The problem Japam solves is real and well-documented: **80–90% of Rudraksha sold online is counterfeit.** Consumers know this, which creates paralysis — they want authentic products but have no reliable way to verify. Japam's lab-testing infrastructure converts this anxiety into a purchasing decision.

Key competitors include Rudra Centre, Prabhubhakti, and Soultheory — but none have Japam's scale (35K monthly customers), growth velocity, or systematic authentication infrastructure.

The target demographic — urban professionals aged 25–45 seeking spirituality as lifestyle rather than ritual — is large, affluent, and underserved by traditional temple-adjacent sellers.

### Shark's Logic

**Namita Thapar** invested because Japam demonstrated what she values most: operational rigor behind an emotional category. The BTR system, lab testing, and vendor qualification processes showed a business built on systems, not just sentiment. Her Emcure background means she understands how trust-dependent categories (health, wellness) require proof infrastructure — Japam built exactly that.

**Varun Alagh** saw the D2C playbook he executed at Mamaearth replaying in a new category. Strong margins, repeat purchase behavior (spiritual products are consumed regularly — malas wear out, new designs launch), micro-influencer-driven discovery, and a product that solves a genuine consumer pain point. The ₹30+ crore ARR in under three years of operation validated product-market fit before the Shark Tank check was written.

### Takeaways

**Trust is the product in unorganized markets.** Japam doesn't just sell Rudraksha — it sells *verified* Rudraksha. The BTR system is the real innovation, not the beads themselves. In any category where authenticity is uncertain, the brand that institutionalizes verification wins.

**Serial entrepreneurs bring compounding advantages.** Ritoban's 19 years of building businesses — websites, SaaS platforms, learning platforms — meant he already knew how to acquire customers online, build systems, and scale operations. Japam benefited from every lesson those prior businesses taught him. First-time founders in the same space would take years longer to reach the same scale.

**Spirituality commerce is a legitimate, scalable market.** Viraj's ethical hesitation reflects a real tension, but the $58 billion market size and Japam's ₹30+ crore ARR answer the commercial question clearly. Faith-adjacent categories — astrology apps, spiritual products, temple services — are among India's fastest-growing consumer segments.

**Valuation at ask is rare — and means something.** Japam closed at ₹150 crore without a haircut. That happens when the numbers leave no room for negotiation. Founders should note: transparent, impressive metrics remove the Sharks' primary leverage in deal-making.

## PITCH 3: ZEN BAREFOOT

### Business Snapshot

Zen Barefoot is India's first barefoot shoe company — shoes engineered to mimic the natural movement of walking without shoes while still protecting feet from terrain and debris. The brand is built on a simple scientific premise: the more cushioning a shoe provides, the more it dampens the sensory feedback your feet send to your brain, the more unnatural your gait becomes, and the higher your injury risk over time.

The product lineup includes shoes designed for training, running, and daily urban wear. Pricing sits in the ₹4,499–₹6,999 range — positioning Zen Barefoot as a considered purchase rather than an impulse buy. Post-Shark Tank, the brand runs a discount code (SHARKY15) and markets itself prominently as "As Seen On Shark Tank."

The brand's origin is intensely personal: founder Sahib was a marathon runner who got injured repeatedly despite wearing top-of-the-line ₹20,000 running shoes. After visiting physiotherapists and researching rehabilitation practices in the US, he discovered that rehab professionals universally wore minimalist, zero-cushion barefoot shoes. The injury wasn't bad luck — it was the shoes.

### Founders

**Sahib Aggarwal** — graduate of **BITS Pilani** (one of India's premier engineering institutions), Delhi-based, and a former competitive marathon runner. His LinkedIn reveals a reflective, philosophically-minded founder who writes candidly about failure, product challenges, and the early days of building Zen Barefoot — including flying to Ahmedabad to sell products he didn't yet have in hand.

**Sahib's Father** — co-pitched alongside Sahib, providing operational and business experience. The father-son pairing echoed VKYD's structure earlier in the same episode.

The pitch included a live demonstration: Sahib performed a **cartwheel** while wearing Zen Barefoot shoes on the Tank floor — a visceral, memorable way to prove the shoes' flexibility and range of motion. It became the episode's standout physical demonstration moment.

### Numbers That Matter

| Metric | Figure |
|---|---|
| Market position | India's first barefoot shoe brand |
| Price range | ₹4,499 – ₹6,999 |
| Target use cases | Training, running, daily urban wear |
| Instagram followers | ~9,900 (at time of research) |
| Ask | ₹1 crore for 5% equity (₹20 crore valuation) |
| Scientific backing | US-based studies on barefoot locomotion |

Specific revenue figures were not publicly disclosed. The relatively modest social media presence (~10K followers) and early-stage Instagram activity suggest Zen Barefoot is still in brand-building phase rather than having achieved significant scale at the time of pitch.

### Deal Outcome: NO DEAL ❌

Sharks appreciated the science, the personal origin story, and Sahib's demonstrable conviction (the cartwheel). But they declined, citing three core concerns: **pricing challenges at scale, differentiation in a competitive footwear market, and difficulty balancing sustainability claims with mass-market adoption.**

The barefoot shoe category has a history of struggling on Shark Tank globally — US brand Xero Shoes pitched on the American Shark Tank in 2013 and also walked away without a deal, though the brand grew substantially from the exposure alone.

### Key Business Terms

**Barefoot Shoes:** Footwear designed with ultra-thin, flexible soles and a toe-box shape that allows toes to spread naturally — replicating the biomechanics of walking barefoot while protecting against punctures and weather. The category challenges the entire premise of conventional shoe design (cushioning = comfort = protection) with evidence that cushioning actually distorts natural gait.

**Zero Drop:** A technical term in footwear meaning the heel and forefoot are at the same height — no elevation difference. Conventional running shoes typically have 8–12mm "drop" (heel higher than forefoot). Zero drop forces the foot into a more natural position but requires significant adaptation time for habitual shoe-wearers.

**Transition Risk:** The biggest barrier to barefoot shoe adoption. Feet and leg muscles atrophy from years of cushioned shoe dependency. Switching to barefoot shoes too quickly causes pain and micro-injuries. Zen Barefoot's marketing must navigate this carefully — promising health benefits while warning customers to transition gradually. This creates a longer customer journey than most footwear purchases.

### Market Context

The global barefoot/minimalist shoe market is estimated at **$500 million–$1 billion**, growing as fitness culture and biomechanical awareness expand. In India specifically, the category is essentially unopened — no Indian brand has attempted to own this space at scale before Zen Barefoot.

Competitors are almost entirely international: **Vibram FiveFingers** (the category pioneer), **Xero Shoes, Luna, Merrell Vapor Glove.** None have built significant India-specific presence or pricing for the Indian market. This gives Zen Barefoot genuine first-mover advantage in India — but first-mover advantage only matters if the category itself gains traction.

The science supporting barefoot locomotion is real but contested within orthopedic and sports medicine communities. This ambiguity gives Sharks pause — the brand needs to educate consumers and doctors simultaneously, which is expensive and slow.

### Why Sharks Passed

The footwear market is brutal — dominated by Nike, Adidas, Puma, and locally by brands like Campus and Liberty with massive distribution. Zen Barefoot's ₹4,499–₹6,999 pricing positions it as premium, but the customer base willing to pay that for a niche biomechanical shoe in India is limited today. Sharks saw a real product solving a real problem, but couldn't see the path to the scale needed to justify a ₹20 crore valuation at this stage. The sustainability and health messaging, while genuine, didn't yet translate into the customer acquisition numbers that would close the deal.

### Takeaways

**Education-heavy categories require patience and capital.** Barefoot shoes don't sell themselves — every potential customer must first understand why conventional shoes might be harming them. This requires content investment, community building, and sustained marketing spend before revenue scales. It's a longer runway than Sharks typically fund.

**Live demonstration creates memory but doesn't close deals.** The cartwheel was the episode's most physically memorable moment. But product demonstrations prove capability — they don't prove market size or scalability. Sharks fund businesses, not acrobatics.

**First-mover in India ≠ first-mover globally.** Zen Barefoot is India's first barefoot shoe brand, but the category has existed internationally since 2004 (Vibram FiveFingers). Global precedent shows barefoot shoes remain niche — mainstream adoption has been slower than early evangelists predicted. Sharks factor this in when evaluating Indian first-movers in categories that have already struggled to cross the chasm elsewhere.

**Father-son pitches need clearer role definition.** Both VKYD and Zen Barefoot featured father-son duos, and both went home without deals. Sharks want to understand who runs what — and more critically, who will be running the business in five years. When that clarity is missing, it creates uncertainty about long-term leadership.

## EPISODE-LEVEL INSIGHTS

### The Spectrum of "Reinvention"

All three pitches attempted to reinvent familiar categories:

| Brand | Category | Reinvention Attempt | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| **VKYD** | Fashion | Digital-first, phygital delivery | No Deal |
| **Japam** | Spiritual products | Authenticity infrastructure + modern design | ₹1.5 Cr Deal ✅ |
| **Zen Barefoot** | Footwear | Science-backed biomechanical design | No Deal |

The difference wasn't ambition — all three were ambitious. It was **where the market already existed and was ready to pay.** Japam's spiritual products market is $58 billion and growing. VKYD's phygital fashion and Zen Barefoot's barefoot shoes are early-stage categories still proving demand.

### Why Only Japam Got Funded

Three factors separated Japam from the other two pitches:

**1. Proven revenue at scale.** ₹30+ crore ARR with 1,200 daily orders and 35K monthly customers. VKYD had ₹2 crore cumulative (impressive for an 18-year-old, insufficient for a ₹10 crore valuation). Zen Barefoot's revenue was undisclosed — likely early-stage.

**2. Repeatable purchase behavior.** Spiritual products are consumed, gifted, and replaced regularly. Malas wear out. Festivals create natural reorder moments. This creates recurring revenue without expensive re-acquisition. Fashion and footwear purchases are more sporadic.

**3. A structural market problem solved.** 80–90% of Rudraksha online is fake. Japam's BTR system solves this at scale. Neither VKYD nor Zen Barefoot addressed a problem of comparable magnitude in their categories.

### Father-Son Dynamics: A Pattern Worth Noting

Two of three pitches this episode featured father-son teams. Neither secured funding. This isn't coincidence — Sharks are watching these dynamics carefully:

- **Who holds the vision?** If the son has the idea but the father runs operations, what happens when they disagree?
- **Who will be CEO in 5 years?** Investors need confidence in long-term leadership continuity.
- **Is the father's involvement accelerating or slowing the business?** Serial entrepreneurship experience (Sanjay Goel) adds value. But if the father's presence creates deference rather than challenge, it can limit the younger founder's growth.

Japam's Ritoban — a solo, seasoned serial entrepreneur — contrasted sharply. No divided authority, no generational tension, just a founder who knew exactly what he was doing and why.

### Valuation Psychology This Episode

| Brand | Ask Valuation | Outcome | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| VKYD | ₹10 Cr | No Deal | Traction insufficient for valuation |
| Japam | ₹150 Cr | Funded at ask | Numbers left no room to negotiate |
| Zen Barefoot | ₹20 Cr | No Deal | Category risk + early revenue stage |

Japam's ability to close at full ask valuation is the episode's sharpest investment lesson. When ARR is ₹30+ crore and growing rapidly, the Sharks' primary negotiating leverage disappears. Founders with strong numbers should price confidently — Sharks respect it more than they argue with it.

### Post-Episode Trajectories

**Japam** is deploying the ₹1.5 crore toward manufacturing scale, product portfolio expansion, and quality assurance reinforcement. With Namita and Varun's networks now accessible, retail partnerships and brand credibility will compound. The ₹60 crore revenue target for FY25-26 is ambitious but structurally achievable given the growth rate.

**VKYD** likely benefits most from pure visibility. The Shark Tank episode introduced phygital fashion to millions of viewers who'd never encountered the concept. Whether Arjun converts that awareness into business growth depends on Roblox's ecosystem evolution and his ability to expand beyond gaming into broader digital fashion.

**Zen Barefoot** is already leveraging the "As Seen On Shark Tank" tag on its website and running discount codes. The barefoot shoe category has historical precedent for post-show growth (Xero Shoes grew significantly after its US Shark Tank appearance despite no deal). Education-driven categories often benefit more from visibility than from capital.
